# Story Bible — Elementals Unbound

Status: **first draft**. The designer chose the direction on 2026-10-23 (see *Fixed by the designer* below). Names of places and people, and the cast, are Story's proposals and can still change. Every other story file follows this one.

## Fixed by the designer

- **The arc:** the hero starts small-time and rises through the ranks as they gain experience and power. The story is about growth, coming to understand duty, and finally joining the people who were once far above them.
- **The world:** full of magic. Monsters can appear anywhere, at any time. Adventurers take contracts and hunt monsters to protect ordinary people.
- **The world moves on its own:** big events happen far above the hero's head, and many characters are much stronger than them for most of the game.
- **The threat:** a calamity is rising. The nations notice things getting worse and use it as a chance to grab power, land and resources.
- **The turn:** by the end the hero learns that some of the people at the top, people they looked up to, don't share their ideals. The hero overcomes them to protect the world.
- **Cards are bound monsters.**
- **The protagonist is a fixed character** with their own voice and past.
- **Tone:** earnest at heart, with real danger. Contracts go wrong, towns get hit, and some people die.
- **Scale (added 2026-10-23):** a long JRPG-style epic, **30–50 hours**, with lots of character build-up and world-building.
- **Monster ranks:** monsters are ranked the same way as guild members. Normal encounters are usually the player's own rank; bosses are one or two ranks higher.

## Tone in one paragraph

Hopeful, but it costs something. The world is beautiful and busy: market towns, river barges, sky-ports, mountain holds. It also lives with monsters the way coastal towns live with storms. Adventurers are working professionals, not chosen ones. The early game is about small jobs done well: a wolf-thing in the orchard, a flooded mill, a missing courier. The danger is real from the first act, and it climbs with the hero's rank. Humor comes from the people. Weight comes from what the hero chooses to protect. Nobody is evil for its own sake. The final antagonist believes they are saving the world.

Touchstones: **classic BioWare above all** (*Jade Empire*, *Knights of the Old Republic* I and II, *Dragon Age: Origins*, the *Mass Effect* trilogy; designer's favorites, see the next section). Also *Frieren* and *Monster Hunter* for the working-adventurer feel, *Final Fantasy XII* for nations scheming over a crisis, and *Delicious in Dungeon* for dungeons that feel like real places.

## Writing style: the BioWare model (designer, 2026-10-23)

The designer's favorite RPGs are *Jade Empire*, *KOTOR* 1 and 2, *Dragon Age: Origins* and the *Mass Effect* trilogy. We take their **craft**, never their plots, characters or lines. This is how every story file is written.

**The hero is a Shepard.** Wren has a fixed name, history and face (the player picks the gender), like Commander Shepard or the Warden. The player decides **how** Wren answers: kind or blunt, by the book or ruthless, joking or grave. Every main conversation offers 2–4 replies that carry real attitude, not three ways of saying yes. Wren's written voice (earnest, quick) shows in the lines themselves, so every option still sounds like Wren.

**Two ways to hold the line.** A philosophy axis in the spirit of the Open Palm and Closed Fist, or Paragon and Renegade, built on this game's own question: *who do you protect, and what will you spend to do it?*

- **Shield:** protect the people in front of you, keep your word, take the harder road so nobody gets left behind.
- **Edge:** finish the hunt, spend what has to be spent, and let results answer for you.

Both are heroic, both are tracked separately (as in *Mass Effect*), and both open their own options. Seraphine is Edge carried to its end, which makes the final confrontation personal for an Edge player and an argument for a Shield player. Neither path turns Wren into her. The axis shows up in replies, in how contracts are resolved, and in the ending.

**Companions have opinions and argue.** As in *Dragon Age: Origins*:

- Each companion has values that a choice can please or offend, and **approval** moves visibly ("Brann approves").
- They argue with Wren and with each other: banter while walking, camp conversations, and lines reacting to your choices on the spot.
- High approval opens personal talks, loyalty quests and romance. Low approval leads to confrontation, and a companion can leave the party (Pell might go home to the Dominion). Nobody is a yes-man.

**Loyalty quests matter at the end** (from *Mass Effect 2*). Each companion's loyalty quest resolves their arc. The final vault is a *Suicide Mission*-style hunt. Companions hold the side fights (see *Companions in battle*), and who you send where, together with whether they're loyal, decides who comes home. Dove's death in Act III is fixed. After that, deaths depend on the player.

**Gather the hunters** (from *Dragon Age: Origins*' treaties and *Mass Effect 3*'s war assets). Act V calls on everyone Wren helped: branch halls, nations, rival hunters, even bound great wilds. The Covenant who answer are the ones Wren earned across the game, and they change the final battle.

**A mentor who questions the creed** (from *KOTOR 2*'s Kreia). Corvane Ashe retires from the Seven in Act III and becomes Wren's teacher. He's bitter, brilliant and honest, and he pulls apart the Covenant's ideals in every conversation: "Rank is a leash the nations let us hold." He's right often enough to sting. He doesn't betray Wren. His role is to make Wren's own answer to Seraphine earned, not borrowed.

**The betrayal is planted, then paid off** (as *Jade Empire* did with its master). Seraphine is warm and helpful in every scene before the reveal. Plant clues a second playthrough will catch: towns she "couldn't reach in time", records Isa can't find, Halden's silences. The reveal should make the player re-read the whole game.

**Planets, in our terms** (from *KOTOR* and *Mass Effect*). Act II opens up: the party can visit the League, Holdfast and Skyreach **in any order**. Each is a self-contained story with a moral knot at its center, its own vault, and a decision the world remembers.

**Choices come back.** A *Wrex on Virmire*-level decision in each act, plus small ones (spare a poacher, report a branch master, free a bound wild). They return acts later as allies, enemies, letters and changed towns. Story keeps a ledger of every flag and where it pays off.

**Lore the player can dig into.** A codex (the Covenant's bestiary, binder histories, nation dossiers), written in-world and unlocked by contracts, bindings and conversations. Every wild you bind gets an entry.

**How it reads:**

- **Distinct voices.** You should be able to tell who's speaking with the name hidden.
- **Short lines.** Two sentences per line at most.
- **Humor even in bad times.**
- **No villain monologues.** Antagonists explain themselves like people.
- **Every conversation ends** with Wren learning something, choosing something, or changing a relationship.

## The world: Aster

**Aster** is one continent shared by five nations and crossed by elemental currents called **leylines**. Where leylines tangle or tear, raw element pools and takes shape as **wilds**: monsters made of living Fire, Water, Earth, Wind or Lightning. Wilds can form anywhere. Most are small and dumb. Some grow old, clever and huge.

### Monsters (wilds)

- A wild is element given a body and a hunger. It isn't evil; it's weather with teeth. Most wilds go for what feeds their element: Fire wilds go for forges and granaries, Water wilds for wells and harbors, and so on.
- **Rifts** are tears in a leyline. A rift keeps spawning wilds until someone closes it. Closing a rift is high-rank work.
- **Every wild has a rank.** The Covenant grades wilds on the same scale as its hunters (Tin to Exalted; see *Ranks*), and every contract on a notice board names the rank of the wild. The rule of thumb every rookie learns is *hunt your own rank, and never take a boss alone*. A Copper hunter takes Copper contracts; a Copper wild that has grown into a Steel one is how hunters die.
- **Crowned wilds** are monsters that have swallowed a rift's heart. They are much stronger, they regenerate, and they *break the normal rules of a binding duel*. **This is the story reason for every boss mechanic:** a boss is a crowned wild (or a person carrying a crowned binding), and its crown lets it ignore one rule. Every boss names the rule it breaks.

### Cards (bindings)

- A **binding** is a monster defeated and sealed into a card. The craft is called **binding**, and its practitioners are **binders**. Every adventurer is a binder.
- To bind a wild you must beat it. Hunting is how you get cards, so loot is the reward for a hunt.
- A binder fights by **calling** bound monsters and spells out of their deck. Bound wilds obey the binder, but they keep their nature: a bound Fire wild still burns.
- **Basic cards** are ordinary bindings: lesser wilds, and spellwork drawn from bound element.
- **Charge** is the binder's own channel filling as they work one element. Enough charge lets them call a **Manifest**.
- **Manifests** are the great bindings: old, named, powerful wilds. Calling one strains the seal, so a Manifest can be called **once per fight** (the once-per-game rule). Their creatures keep their names, histories and grudges.
- **Purity:** a binder who keeps to one element can hold more great bindings at once, because their channel runs deeper. This is the Manifest-slot bonus (mono 5, dual 4, triple 3). It's common binder wisdom and a point of pride in some nations.
- **Any binder can carry any card.** Bindings don't bond to their owner; skill lives in the binder. That's why rebuilding a deck around a new find costs nothing, and why a great card in a dungeon is worth the trip.
- **Wards:** every binder wears an elemental ward that soaks harm in a duel. When it breaks, the duel is lost. That's starting life, and it differs by element (a Solar ward is the thickest; Storm, Swamp, Ice, Aurora and Quicksilver wards are the thinnest). Ordinary wilds have weaker wards than binders, which is why normal enemies start with less life.
- **Duels are short.** Bindings burn hot and wards break fast, so most fights are over in a few exchanges (the 3-turn rule). Long fights happen only against crowned wilds and great binders.

### Confluences: braiding

- Two bindings of different elements can be **braided** into one confluence card (Fire + Earth → Volcano). Three can be braided into a triple. In game terms this is fusion and the Forge.
- Braiding is skilled, licensed work. Adventurers earn access to the guild's **Forges** with rank, which is why confluences unlock as the hero rises. The first braid the hero makes is a story beat.
- Some wilds are born confluent where leylines cross: steam wilds at hot springs, sand wilds in the dust basins. Confluence regions sit on those crossings.
- In some nations braiding is distrusted. The Pyrrhen call it "muddy fire". That's useful friction, not a law the hero breaks.

### Ancient bindings: why the best cards are in dungeons

About seven hundred years ago, in the **Sundering**, the greatest wilds of the age, the **Primordials**, nearly unmade Aster. The first binders, the **Founding Circle**, sealed them into bindings and hid each one deep under the world, in vaults built to keep anyone from calling them again. Lesser great wilds were sealed the same way across the centuries by binders who died, fled or were forgotten.

So **every dungeon loot card has an origin:** someone bound it, someone hid it, and something guards it, whether a vault warden, the wilds drawn to its power, or the dead binder's own traps. Each dungeon file in `story/regions/` answers three questions about its loot card: who sealed it, why, and what guards it.

### The calamity: the Unbinding

The Founding Circle's seals are failing. The vaults leak, leylines strain, and wilds are appearing more often, stronger, and in places that were safe for generations. Scholars call it **the Unbinding**. Ordinary people call it a bad few years. The top of the guild and the crowns know the truth: if the Primordial seals fail, the Sundering comes back.

The nations each read that as an opportunity:

- Whoever holds the Primordial bindings holds the strongest weapons in the world.
- Borderlands emptied by wilds are land for the taking.
- A nation that "protects" a neighbor's towns from the Unbinding ends up garrisoning them.

The guild is the only power meant to stand above this. Holding to that ideal, and finding out who at the top has quietly let it go, is the spine of the game.

## The Hunters' Covenant (the guild)

The **Covenant** is a guild of adventurers that crosses all borders. It has branch halls in every town big enough for a notice board. It sells contracts, licenses binders, runs the Forges and records every wild it binds. Under its oldest charter it owes allegiance to no crown, and a Covenant binder may cross any border on a hunt. The nations hate that charter and need it.

### Ranks

Rank is the hero's progress. One scale grades both **hunters and wilds**, and it maps onto the card power ladder, so story progress, enemy strength and deck power climb together.

| Rank | Ladder | Hunters of this rank | Wilds of this rank | Decks they field |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Tin** | 1 | Probationary. Errands and pest control. | Pests: grass sprites, ember mice. | Starter |
| **Copper** | 2 | Village contracts, alone or in a pair. | A threat to a farm or a road. | Mono common |
| **Iron** | 3 | Regional work: escorts, nests, small rifts with a team. | A threat to a village. | Mono uncommon |
| **Steel** | 4 | Trusted across a nation. First vault delves. | A threat to a town. | Mono rare |
| **Silver** | 5–6 | Forge licence (dual braids). Border and rift work. | Confluent wilds; a threat to a city. | Dual common / uncommon |
| **Gold** | 7 | Leads teams. Names known in capitals. | Old, clever confluent wilds. | Dual rare / archetype |
| **Adamant** | 8–9 | Triple braids. Crowned-wild hunters. A few dozen alive. | Crowned wilds; a threat to a nation. | Triple common / uncommon |
| **Exalted** | 10 | The **Seven**: the Covenant's highest binders, its council and its legends. | Primordials; a threat to the world. | Triple rare / archetype |

**Encounter rule (designer, 2026-10-23):** normal encounters are usually the hero's own rank. Bosses are one or two ranks higher, with a crown mechanic on top. In the story, a boss is *always* a wild above your grade: the contract that went wrong, the nest whose mother was bigger than the notice said, the vault guardian no one has survived. Beating one is how a hunter earns promotion. Each rank ends on a boss above it, and the promotion follows. (How "a rank or two" turns into ladder steps and boss numbers is for TCG Design and Balance.)

Enemy AI levels 1–5 read as the enemy's skill within their rank: a green Copper hunter, or a veteran one.

### The Seven Exalted (proposed)

For most of the game these are figures far above the hero: names on posters, a cloak glimpsed across a plaza, a battle already over by the time the hero arrives. Some become mentors, one becomes the enemy, and the hero takes a seat among them in the last act.

1. **Seraphine Vael, "the Lantern"** — First of the Seven, Aurora binder, the most beloved person on Aster. She saved the hero's village when the hero was a child. *Secret antagonist* (see the central conflict).
2. **Gorran Stoneheart** — Glacier binder, the oldest of the Seven. Slow, kind and immovable. Descended from Earthbinder Gorr. The hero's eventual champion on the council.
3. **Ysil of the White Reach** — Ice binder, called the Frost Empress. Cold, exact, unswayed by any crown. The hero's hardest test and finally a true ally.
4. **Aeris Calloway** — Tempest binder, Windlord. Brash and charming, and he owes favors to the Skyreach princes. A grey figure: torn between his oath and his homeland.
5. **Neris Saltwind** — Hurricane binder, Tide Sovereign of the League. Her loyalty is bought by her city. She sides with Seraphine.
6. **Corvane Ashe** — Ash binder. Quiet and haunted, he fought in the last border war. Retires in Act III, opening the seat the hero later fills, and becomes Wren's mentor: the one who questions every ideal (see *Writing style*).
7. **Halden Mirr** — Quicksilver binder, the Covenant's spymaster. Knows more than he says; he is the one who tells the hero the truth.

### The party (proposed)

A 30–50 hour JRPG needs a travelling party, people whose lives the player learns over dozens of hours. Wren's hunting crew is five companions, **one from each nation**. Every nation's turn in the war is personal for someone at the campfire, and each companion's home is a region the party visits with them. Each one has a personal arc with its own chapter, a loyalty quest and a rank-up beside Wren. (Companions never duel directly; they take on the extra monsters while Wren fights the main one. See *Companions in battle*.)

| Companion | From | Element | Who they are | Arc |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Pell Varrane** | Pyrrhen Dominion | Fire | Second son of a war-house, a gifted and arrogant rival rookie, impatient to rise. | Rival in Act I, joins in Act II. When Vexis marches, his family calls him home and he has to choose between blood and oath. He chooses the Covenant, and his brother doesn't. |
| **Isa Corran** | Tidewater League (Saltmere) | Water | Covenant archivist, sharp and bookish, out of her depth in the field. | Works out what the Unbinding is long before anyone listens. | **Brann Holloway** | Kingdom of Holdfast | Earth | A big, gentle Copper hunter far from his mountains, married to Dove (a Copper Wind hunter who travels with them). The party's heart and its comic relief. | Dove dies in Act III, holding a rift so a town can flee. Brann's arc is grief and choosing to keep protecting strangers. He is the human face of "ordinary adventurers". |
| **Kes Arden** | Skyreach Principalities | Wind | A courier-hunter who knows every sky-road, quick-tongued and charming. Secretly a disinherited Skyreach prince. | His family is one of the courts buying Exalted champions. He has to reclaim his name to stop Skyreach from selling out to Seraphine. |

Recurring cast who don't travel with the party:

- **Mads Orrin** — a retired Iron hunter with a bad leg who runs the branch hall in Brindle Ford. Gruff, fair, the first person to believe in the hero. He dies at the end of Act II defending the Ford during a surge, and his death is the hero's first real lesson in duty.
- **Tilly** — a village kid in the Ford who wants to be Wren the way Wren wanted to be Seraphine. She grows up across the game (the long timeline lets that show) and mirrors the hero's arc from below.
- **Branch masters, rivals and contract-givers** in each region, who recur as Wren rises, so the world remembers you.

### Antagonists and powers

- **Seraphine Vael** — the final antagonist. See below.
- **Vexis, the Pyrelord** — Warlord-Regent of the Pyrrhen Dominion, and the plainest villain on the world stage. He uses the Unbinding as cover to march into wild-struck borderlands "to protect them". He's dangerous, open about what he wants, and ultimately someone the hero can deal with.
- **The Hollow** — a cult that believes the Sundering should come back and that wilds are the world's rightful owners. They break seals on purpose. They're the mid-game threat behind many vault breaches, and Seraphine's unwitting tool.
- Crowned wilds and Primordials: the bosses (see the outline).

## The five nations

Each nation leans toward an element without being only that element. Every element appears everywhere, but each land has a home element and its own wilds.

| Nation | Home element | Seat | What it wants from the Unbinding |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The Pyrrhen Dominion** | Fire | Cindergate | Land. The wild-struck borderlands of its neighbors. |
| **The Tidewater League** | Water | Saltmere | Trade and leverage. It controls the rivers and the ports, and sells protection. |
| **The Kingdom of Holdfast** | Earth | Deepmoot | Safety behind its walls. It wants the Primordial vaults under its mountains to stay its own. |
| **The Skyreach Principalities** | Wind | Highcourt | Prestige. Its princes compete to buy Covenant champions. |
| **The Stormcourt of Vol** | Lightning | Thunderhold | Power. A storm-priesthood that wants the Primordial bindings for its god. |

Between them lies **the Hearthvale**: river farmland and market towns, nobody's capital and everybody's road. The hero starts here. It's the first region struck hard by the Unbinding, and the first land the nations eye.

Confluence regions sit where two nations' leylines cross (Steam springs on the League–Dominion border, the Sand basins between Holdfast and Skyreach, and so on). They're contested ground, which is where the war happens.

## The hero

**Wren Calder**, 18 (proposed name). The player picks Wren's gender at the start. Wren's history and voice are fixed, and the player shapes Wren's attitude through dialogue choices and the Shield/Edge axis, as with Shepard.

**Cold open:** a playable flashback to the night of the fire, eight-year-old Wren in the burning Ford, ending as Seraphine walks out of the smoke. It runs five minutes before the title card. The player will remember it when she turns.

- **Origin:** a miller's child from **Brindle Ford** in the Hearthvale. At eight, Wren watched a crowned Fire wild burn half the Ford until Seraphine Vael walked in and ended it in one call. Wren has wanted to be her ever since.
- **Start:** a fresh Tin-rank binder with one secondhand starter deck and more nerve than skill. Not special. No destiny, no hidden bloodline. Wren rises by working.
- **Voice:** earnest, quick, a bit too proud of small wins. Talks to bound monsters as if they can hear (maybe they can). Gets quieter and steadier as the game goes on.
- **Flaw:** hero-worship. Wren believes the people at the top are better people, not just stronger ones. The whole arc tests that belief.
- **Ideal (what Wren comes to understand):** the Covenant exists for the people who can't hold a card. Rank is a debt owed to them, not a reward.
- **Element:** none fixed. Wren is a generalist by necessity (secondhand cards) and later by choice. That fits the free deck switching. The starter deck choice can be framed as "the deck Mads lends you".

### Arc in four beats

1. **Small-time** — proves they can do the job. Learns that contracts are about people.
2. **Rising** — sees the world is bigger and worse than they knew. Loses Mads. Chooses duty over glory.
3. **Among the great** — fights beside the Exalted in the war and the rift-surges. Earns their respect. Starts to see the cracks.
4. **One of them** — takes a seat among the Seven. Sees that Seraphine's plan would save the world by ruling it. Overcomes their hero.

## Shape of the game: 30–50 hours

A prologue and five acts. The **main story runs about 30 hours**, and contracts, companion quests and optional vaults bring it to **about 50**. Each act covers a rank band and ends on a boss one or two ranks above the party, followed by the promotion. Hours are targets for pacing, not promises.

**JRPG pacing rules for the outline:**

- **Every region is a place, not a level.** It has a town with a branch hall and its own notice board, people who remember you, local politics, a vault with a sealed great wild and its history, and a reason to come back later.
- **Return to Brindle Ford in every act.** Show it changing with the war and the Unbinding, and show Tilly growing up. Home is how the player measures how far they've come.
- **The world moves without you.** Each act opens with news of what happened while you were busy (a broadsheet, a branch-hall rumour, a refugee column). Exalted are seen doing things far beyond you, and you arrive after the fact.
- **Companion chapters.** Each companion gets a chapter in their home region and an optional loyalty quest that ends on a boss and a personal loot card.
- **Camp scenes.** Short party conversations at rest points after big beats. This is where most of the character build-up lives.
- **Side contracts** on every notice board, ranked like the wilds they name. They're the main source of the extra 20 hours, and many of them pay off in later acts.

## Central conflict

**On the surface:** the Unbinding is getting worse, and the nations are circling.

**Underneath:** Seraphine Vael has known for years that the Founding seals can't hold. Her answer is to gather the Primordial bindings before the Hollow or any crown can, rebind them all into herself, and become the new seal: one keeper, with the power of the Sundering, deciding which lands are worth protecting. She has quietly let border towns fall so that the nations, terrified, will hand her the vaults. She believes the cost is worth it. Neris backs her for her city's sake. Others on the council half-know and look away.

**The hero's answer:** the seals should be renewed the way the Founding Circle made them, by many binders and not one, and nobody gets to choose which villages burn. The final act is Wren gathering the binders Seraphine overlooked (the party, Covenant hunters of every rank, Ysil and Gorran) and stopping her at the last vault.

**What makes it land:** Seraphine is right about the danger and kind in person. She is the one who saved Wren. The final duel is against the person Wren became an adventurer to be.

## Story-to-system fixed points

These are the world facts other roles build on. Each will be logged as a board decision.

- **Bosses are crowned wilds or crowned binders. Each crown breaks one named rule** (examples: extra life bars as phases, a second Manifest call, unblockable attacks, the boss calls from your charge). TCG Design designs the mechanic, Balance sets the numbers, and Story gives the reason.
- **One rank scale for hunters and wilds, mapped to the power ladder** (table above). Normal encounters are the hero's rank; bosses are one or two ranks higher. Each act ends on such a boss, followed by a promotion.
- **Each companion has a home region, an element and a personal loot card** (see *The party*).
- **Confluences unlock by Forge licence at Silver rank, and triples at Adamant.** Story supplies the licence moments.
- **Every dungeon has a loot card with an origin** (who sealed it, why, what guards it).
- **Enemy life is lower than the player's because wilds wear thinner wards than binders.**

## Naming rules

- Places: English compounds with a weathered feel (Brindle Ford, Saltmere, Cindergate). Capitals get one strong word.
- People: short given names, plain surnames, and earned epithets ("the Lantern"). Exalted have epithets; rookies don't.
- Wilds and bindings: descriptive card names as they are now. Named great wilds keep a name and a history.
- **Open question:** a few cards use real-world gods' names (Raijin, Raiden, Gaia, Atlas, the Thunder God). The proposal is to rename them into Aster's own legends (Vol, the storm god of the Stormcourt; Earthbinder Gorr; and so on) in a later card-names PR, which Balance reviews.

## Companions in battle (designer, 2026-10-23)

**Companions don't duel directly. Wren is always the one at the table.** When a fight has more than one monster, the companions take on the others while Wren focuses on the main one. In the story this is how a Covenant crew hunts: the lead binder takes the big wild, and the crew keeps its pack, its brood or the rift's spawn off them.

- This gives every multi-monster scene a reason to have the party there. "Brann, Dove: hold the brood. Wren, the mother's yours."
- Companions can be seen and heard during the fight (barks, cut-ins) and can win or struggle with their side of it. A companion's fight going badly is a natural story beat; Dove's death in Act III is the crew holding a second front too long.
- How the side fights show up in the game (pure story, a timer, an effect on Wren's battle, a result after the battle) is a mechanics question for TCG Design and Game Dev.

## Romance (designer, 2026-10-23)

Some romance options, optional and built from camp scenes and loyalty quests. Proposal:

- **Who can be romanced:** Pell, Isa, Kes and Tamsin. Brann is married to Dove, so his bond is friendship and, after Act III, grief.
- **How it works:** approval grows through camp conversations, choices and loyalty quests, as with *Dragon Age: Origins*' camp romances. In Act III Wren can commit to one of them, or to none. A committed romance changes camp scenes, at least one key scene per act from then on, and the ending.
- **What it adds to the story:** each romance raises the stakes in that companion's arc. Pell's choice between his family and his oath, Isa exposing her family, Kes reclaiming his crown, Tamsin facing her god: in each case Wren has a personal reason to stand by them.
- **Tone:** sincere and slow-burning, in keeping with the game's earnest tone. Nothing explicit.
- **Wren's gender (designer, 2026-10-23): the player picks at the start.** Same character, voice and story; the choice sets the portrait, the sprite and the pronouns. All four romances are open whichever the player picks. Dialogue that names Wren's gender uses a pronoun token for Game Dev to fill in.
